Symphony on Highway 17

Personal essay

On a beautiful day in August, three of my high school friends and I took a girls’ trip down to Santa Cruz. The drive down on Highway 17 put everyone on edge, but we all loosened our grip on the steering wheel a bit on the drive back. The four of us had met at different times between elementary and high school, through various mutual friends and close proximity. We bonded over classes, extracurriculars, and AP scores, all of which defined our college applications and the Bay Area Gen-Z culture. The papers we turned in for AP Literature held the same buzz words, transition phrases, and crumbs of the most-used Thesaurus website. The digital applications we sent to the communal list of acceptable colleges were different fonts of the same text, sparkled with our unique personalities that weren’t as unique as they felt to each of us. We ate fruit from the same supermarkets, and, therefore, the same crops, and maybe even the same plant. We spoke with the same twang, our sentences went up at the end when we got excited, and we all gossiped in exasperation when our teacher said something outrageous to our tiny minds.

When we went our separate ways to begin our college journeys, my Bay Area accent made me unique among the so-called “Diegans” of San Diego, the rich internationals, and the rare Midwesterners that we were always so curious to meet. They used weird words that sounded foreign yet SAT-level, but ones my hometown friends and I would never use in conversation. My feet had never seen the world outside of my basic white Reeboks, much less the same sand that the nude old men and surfers on Blacks Beach tread on. My street fashion put me out of place, and my thin sunglasses made me look cocky rather than the “cool and mysterious” I was going for. As the school year progressed, I befriended the Diegans and the Midwesterners—even the scary internationals that sat next to me in class—and swapped out my sleek sneakers for breezy sandals. The ends of my sentences turned downward in excitement, and now I made jokes that would’ve caused my high school self to lose her eyes behind her head.

Back on Highway 17 on the way back to our parents’ houses, my high school friends and I chatted as the sun set behind us. For a moment, I realized our conversation no longer sounded like the single note that defined us in high school. We talked at different cadences, used different words, giggled at different parts of the story, laughed at different pitches. We came from the same place but had all gone in different directions, picking up the pebbles and stones from the new places we’ve been and the people we’ve met. For a second, I was sad that my closest high school friend had replaced her familiar slang with the alleged most popular Illinois filler phrase, “Let’s be so for real,” but listening to the four of us harmonize with our voices and experiences, I was so immensely grateful. Grateful to the people and places that opened themselves to us, got to know us, and allowed us to become a part of them. Maybe we had all lost a bit of our Bay Area-ness, but we had become a little more Diegan, Illinoisan, and a little more ourselves.
